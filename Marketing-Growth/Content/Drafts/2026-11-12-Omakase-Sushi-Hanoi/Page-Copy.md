# Omakase and Sushi in Hanoi: A Traveler's Guide (2026)

Five days of pho is a great trip. On day six, you might want something cold, clean and quiet.

Hanoi has more Japanese food than most travelers expect. A large Japanese business community has lived here since the mid-2000s, and the city now has everything from MICHELIN-listed chef's counters to cheap ramen in a back lane.

## What omakase means (and what to expect)

*Omakase* means "I leave it to you." You sit at a counter. The chef decides what you eat, in what order, often one piece at a time.

What a typical evening looks like:

- **Length:** about 1.5 to 2.5 hours.
- **Courses:** usually 10 to 20 small courses, mostly sushi, with a few cooked dishes and a dessert.
- **Seats:** often 8 to 14 at the counter. That is why booking matters.
- **Timing:** many counters run fixed seatings. Arrive on time. A late guest holds up everyone.

If you have never done omakase before, a lunch set is the cheapest way to try it.

## Casual sushi and ramen in Hanoi's Japanese quarter

The streets around **Linh Lang, Kim Mã and Đào Tấn** in Ba Dinh are the closest thing Hanoi has to a Japantown. Japanese companies started posting staff here in the mid-2000s, and the restaurants followed.

Come in the evening. The lanes fill with izakaya lanterns, office workers and the smell of grilled skewers.

Places to start [VERIFY all with Paul]:

- **Takumi**, 95 Linh Lang: one of the city's older Japanese restaurants, known for sushi and sashimi.
- **Sushibar**, 289 Kim Mã: easy, no-booking sushi.

- **Ramen Haron**, Lane 40 Linh Lang: a small family ramen shop with a lunch crowd.
- **Sushi Aoyama Hanoi**, 15 Đào Tấn: a sushi and omakase restaurant on the edge of the quarter.

**Getting there:** the Japanese quarter is about 4 km west of the Old Quarter. A Grab car takes about 15–25 minutes, depending on traffic. [VERIFY]

## Omakase etiquette in 60 seconds

1. **Tell them about allergies when you book**, not when the first piece lands.
2. **Eat each piece soon after it is served.** The rice is at its best for a few seconds.
3. **Hands or chopsticks are both fine** for nigiri.
4. **Go easy on soy sauce.** Many pieces are already seasoned.
5. **Skip strong perfume.** You are sitting close to the food and other guests.
6. **Ask before you photograph the chef.** Most say yes. Some counters say no.

## How to photograph omakase without being that guest

We are photographers, so here is the honest advice:

- **No flash. Ever.** It ruins the mood and the photo. Counter lighting is usually warm and directional, which is all you need.
- **Shoot from your seat, at plate level.** Standing up to get the "top-down" shot blocks the people next to you.
- **Take one photo, then eat.** Fifteen seconds is enough. Cold rice is a bad trade for a better angle.
- **The best frame is often the chef's hands**, not the fish. Ask first, then wait for the moment they press the rice.
- **Turn off the shutter sound** if your phone allows it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does omakase cost in Hanoi?
Most omakase dinners we found cost about 1,500,000 to 4,000,000 VND ($58–$155) per person before drinks, service charge and VAT. Special event menus can reach 10,000,000 VND. [VERIFY]

### Do I need to book omakase in Hanoi?
Yes. Counters are small and many run fixed seatings. Book a few days ahead, more for weekends. [VERIFY with restaurants]

### Where is the Japanese area in Hanoi?
Around Linh Lang, Kim Mã and Đào Tấn streets in Ba Dinh district, about 4 km west of the Old Quarter.

### Is there good cheap sushi in Hanoi?
Yes. Casual sushi bars in the Japanese quarter serve sets well under $20. [VERIFY]

### Is it safe to eat raw fish in Hanoi?
Choose busy, specialist Japanese restaurants with high turnover. The restaurants in this guide focus on sushi and imported fish. If you have a sensitive stomach, start with cooked dishes or a beef omakase.
